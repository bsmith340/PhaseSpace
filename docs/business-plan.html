Section 5

Strategy

Win one small market completely, make money from people first and software second, and let your own results do the selling.

Positioning

For CCEA A level students in Northern Ireland who need to understand Maths, Further Maths or Physics properly, BcsMaths is the complete free course that follows the specification point by point, with explanations, live labs and self-marking practice. Unlike general UK revision sites, it's built for CCEA and nothing else, by a student who learned it the hard way.

  • Peers. You're in the same year group as your customers, so you can see what's hard and what's coming up in real time.

Pricing logic

  • Groups beat 1:1 on your time. 8 students × £30 over 5 hours is £48 per hour of your time; 1:1 is £20. Use 1:1 to fill gaps and get reviews, and put your effort into groups.
  • Start low, raise on proof. You're a sixth-former competing with graduates. £20 is fair now. Results and reviews let you charge more later.
  • Annual Pro is the main option. £29 a year is about 6 months of the monthly price. Students think in school years, so sell "the year until your exams".
  • No discounts without a reason: siblings, a 5-hour block or early Easter booking only.
  • Refunds: full refund before a course starts; after that, a credit for the next course. Put this in writing before anyone pays.

What a good session or course must deliver

  1. A clear goal for that session ("You can do any CCEA SHM question by the end").
  2. Practice that marks itself, set on BcsMaths, with scores you can see.
  3. A two-line progress note to the parent: what was covered, what's next, the drill score.
  4. At the end of a course, one timed mock and a before-and-after score. That's your review material (section 8).

Section 8

Reviews and social proof

Parents buy on trust, and reviews are how you earn it. UK law now governs how you collect them, so do it properly from the first one.

The law, in short

Since 6 April 2025, the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024 bans fake reviews outright. That includes writing them, paying or rewarding someone for a review without making that clear, and presenting reviews in a misleading way (for example, hiding the bad ones). Businesses that show reviews must take reasonable steps to stop fake ones. Penalties can reach 10% of global turnover. The CMA's guidance is CMA208.

How to get real reviews

  1. Ask at the high point. Right after a course's final mock, when the before-and-after score is on screen, or right after results day.
  2. Ask everyone, not just the happy ones. Asking only satisfied customers counts as cherry-picking.
  3. Make it 60 seconds. Three questions: what was hard before, what changed, would you recommend it and to whom.
  4. Get permission to publish, and how they want to be named: first name and school year, or anonymous. Parents give permission for under-18s.
  5. Keep the evidence: every review saved with its date and source.
  6. Show the average honestly. If you show a rating, show the number of reviews next to it, and don't remove fair negatives.

Where to put them

Three short quotes on the home and tutoring pages; a before-and-after mock score on course pages (with permission); a Google Business Profile so parents searching locally see real reviews.

Other proof that counts as much

ProofHow to get it
Teacher spot-checkAsk 2–3 teachers to review one unit each for accuracy. With permission: "Unit checked by a Head of Maths."
Public error logA short "Fixed this week" list. Shows you care about accuracy and makes reporting errors normal.
Usage numbersOnce analytics run: "Used by 1,200 CCEA students this term". Only real, current numbers.
Your resultsAfter results day, only if you're comfortable sharing, and only what's true.

Never

  • Write reviews yourself, or get friends or family to write ones that aren't based on real use.
  • Offer discounts, freebies or entry into a prize draw in return for a positive review. Any incentive must be disclosed, and no incentive should depend on the rating.
  • Swap reviews with other tutors or sites.
  • Show only the five-star ones, or invent a rating.

Section 13

What not to do

Most small businesses don't fail from doing too little. They fail from doing the wrong things with confidence. Read this list before every big decision.

  1. Don't let the business cost you your grades. Your results are the brand. Stick to the time budget in section 12, especially from April to June.
  2. Don't build features before you sell. The site already has more than enough. The next £1K comes from people and promotion, not code.
  3. Don't put the free course behind a paywall. Free is how students find you. Charge for extras, never for what's free today.
  4. Don't fake, buy, swap or reward reviews. It's been illegal since 6 April 2025, and one exposed fake review destroys trust in a small community where everyone talks.
  5. Don't host CCEA past papers or mark schemes. Link to CCEA's site. Their papers are their copyright.
  6. Don't use the CCEA logo or imply they endorse you. Keep the "not affiliated" notice.
  7. Don't charge for Pro before accounts and sync exist. Selling a subscription whose progress can vanish when someone clears their browser means refunds and anger.
  8. Don't take payments in your own name if you're under 18. Set up Stripe with a parent as account owner, and keep records from day one.
  9. Don't promise grades. "Guaranteed A*" is misleading and you can't control it. Promise structure, practice and honest feedback.
  10. Don't spend money on ads yet. No paid ads until you have 10 paying customers who found you for free, and know which message made them buy.
  11. Don't be on every platform. Your school network plus one short-video app, done every week, beats seven apps done badly.
  12. Don't underprice or give everything away in DMs. Free help that turns into hours of unpaid tutoring by message burns your time. Point people to the site or a session.
  13. Don't expand to other exam boards or GCSE content. Win CCEA A level first. Focus is your edge against the big sites.
  14. Don't collect data you don't need, or track without a privacy notice. Your users are children, so the bar is higher.
  15. Don't publish AI-written or unchecked content. One confident wrong answer in front of a teacher costs more than ten new pages gain.
  16. Don't rebrand in the middle of the school year. Keep "BcsMaths" through June 2027 and decide once, in October 2027, whether a wider name is worth the cost.
  17. Don't sell to schools without proof. Teachers buy evidence: usage numbers, accuracy checks, other teachers' opinions. Pilot free first.
  18. Don't run a course that hasn't filled. Set a minimum (5 students). Below it, refund, or turn it into 1:1 sessions.
  19. Don't host chat or forums for under-18s. Moderating them is a safeguarding responsibility you can't cover alone.
  20. Don't measure vanity. Views and followers don't pay. Track visitors, sign-ups, bookings and revenue.
  21. Don't let the old bcs35 site die without the redirect. Existing users and their progress need a path across. It's already built: deploy it.

Section 14

Risks and how to handle them

RiskChanceImpactWhat you do about it
Business time eats into revisionHighHighHard weekly cap (section 12). Track study hours on the scorecard. Pause everything in exam season.
Nobody finds the siteHighHighTwo channels done every week; landing pages in summer; teachers as multipliers.
An error in the content embarrasses youMediumHigh"Report an error" button, teacher spot-checks, a public fix log, fixes within 24 hours.
Copyright complaint over spec quotesLowHighAsk CCEA for permission or reword before charging. Never host papers.
A course doesn't fillMediumLowMinimum 5 students; announce 4–6 weeks early; refund or move to 1:1.
Safeguarding concern in tutoringLowVery highParent consent, transparent channels, AccessNI check, no private in-person meetings, written session rules.
Payment or tax mistakesMediumMediumParent on the account; same-day record keeping; register with HMRC once past £1,000.
A big site launches CCEA contentLowMediumYour edges stay: labs, focus, community and price. Move faster on landing pages and reviews.
Losing the site or codeLowHighFix GitHub access so the code is backed up off your laptop; keep the deploy zips.
Burnout (around 70-hour weeks)MediumHighSunday is the only planning slot; batch content; drop the weakest channel every quarter.

Early warning signs

  • Two weeks in a row over your business-hours cap: cut tutoring slots first.
  • Visitors flat for four weeks: change the content format, not the volume.
  • A course below 50% booked two weeks out: message past customers and teachers directly, or postpone.
  • Any message from a parent about safety or conduct: reply the same day, calmly, in writing, and get advice if needed.

Decision rules

  • Build or sell? If it doesn't bring in users or money within 8 weeks, sell instead.
  • Say yes to a new idea? Only if you drop something of equal size.
  • Raise prices? When you're fully booked for 3 weeks running, or a course sells out in under a week.
  • Spend money? Only if it's under £20 and saves an hour a week, or a customer is already waiting for it.
